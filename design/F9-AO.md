# F9 — ambient occlusion that looks like occlusion

This record covers roadmap item F9 (2026-11-12). It says what changed, why the result reads as light being blocked rather than as a gradient pasted on the geometry, and how each claim was verified, including the places where the numbers are weaker than the headline.

## What was wrong

Joel reviewed the F8 corner probe and his read was exact: the screen-space GTAO pass darkens a band of about the same width along every edge and seam, which reads as a gradient pasted on the geometry rather than light being blocked. That is what screen-space occlusion is — it knows the depth and normals of the current frame and nothing about the actual room — so the fix is not a better tuning of the same pass. The fix is to compute static occlusion from the real geometry once, at scene build time, and to demote the screen-space pass to the one job it is good at, contact darkening for things that move.

## Part 1 — baked occlusion for static scenes

The bake lives in src/PhotonicBakedAO.js, driven from the scene as scene.bakeAmbientOcclusion(options), with scene.clearBakedAmbientOcclusion() to undo it and scene.getBakedAOInfo() to report it. At bake time the scene's world transforms are settled, and every created object that is a mesh becomes both a receiver and an occluder, except handles named in the bake's exclude list, which do neither (that list is how moving objects stay out of the bake). Occlusion is computed by ray casting: for every vertex of every receiving mesh, twenty directions are cast over the hemisphere around the vertex normal against double-sided stand-in meshes that carry the geometry exactly as authored, and a hit counts against the vertex in proportion to how close it lands inside the bake radius (2.2 metres by default), so geometry that is near and large blocks a lot of sky, geometry at the edge of the radius only shades a little, and a vertex with nothing around it stays fully open. The sample directions are a fixed Fibonacci hemisphere, so the same scene bakes to the same occlusion on any machine, in the spirit of the Vomitorium project's baked arena (which bakes per-vertex occlusion from its generated geometry at pre-generation time and is the in-house precedent for how much more legitimate this reads). The result is written into the vertex colour channel with the material cloned per mesh and vertex colours switched on, and a visibility floor of 0.22 keeps the deepest corner from going black.

Two pieces of engineering make the per-vertex form actually work on this engine's geometry. The first is tessellation: the plane and box creators emit a handful of vertices (a plane has four), which gives per-vertex occlusion nowhere to live, so the bake first subdivides authored planes and boxes at a 0.3 metre step (capped per axis) while keeping the original geometry aside for the ray casts and for clearing; spheres and cylinders already carry enough vertices and are left alone. The second is the default floor: the grid's floor plane is a single 10,000-metre plane that can never be tessellated, and it is the floor the standard examples stand on, so its contact patches are baked separately into a transparent ground texture — a 128 by 128 grid of ray-cast floor points sized to the occluders' bounds plus the bake radius, drawn as a black texture whose alpha is the occlusion, laid just above the floor. That is what puts a patch that matches the box's actual footprint under the box in the box example, where there is no authored floor at all.

The options, all with the defaults above unless set, are radius, samples, intensity, minVisibility, tessellation, groundTexture, groundResolution, and groundSamples, plus the exclude list. A bake is a build-time step, not a frame step: after it runs, the static occlusion costs nothing per frame beyond the tessellated geometry and one transparent ground quad. When the layout changes, the scene is re-baked; there is no attempt to track edits automatically.

## Part 2 — the screen-space pass, retuned down to dynamic contact

The GTAO chain in src/PhotonicRenderer.js keeps its F8 shape (render pass, GTAO pass, output pass, opt-in, steps aside in XR) and changes its defaults and its limits. The radius grows from the stock 0.25 to 0.9, so contact shading reaches as far as a contact patch should, and distanceExponent and distanceFallOff are set so the effect fades with distance instead of ending at a hard edge. The pass's own Poisson denoise — present in the chain since F8 but left at its stock settings — is now driven explicitly and wider (denoiseRadius 14 against the stock 8, denoiseSamples 24 against 16), so what survives is a soft shade, never a hard band. The blend strength defaults to 0.55 instead of 1, the GTAO scale drops to 0.9, and there is a hard ceiling: blendIntensity is clamped to 0.85 wherever it is applied, so no tuning a consumer passes in can make the dynamic pass crush a contact toward black; getPostProcessingInfo reports the ceiling alongside the options. The denoise controls are exposed as PostProcessingOptions fields (denoiseRadius, denoiseSamples, denoiseRings, denoiseRadiusExponent) for scenes that want to tune them.

## Part 3 — the balance in the defaults

Nothing about the defaults lets occlusion do the grounding work. Real shadows (F7) are on by default and remain the main grounding; grading (F8) is on by default and unchanged; the baked occlusion is a deliberate build-time call a scene makes when its layout is static; and the screen-space chain stays opt-in and frame-costly, now tuned as garnish. A scene that does none of this looks exactly as it did after F8.

## Verification

Everything below ran headlessly in Chromium with software rendering (SwiftShader) at 820 by 640, serving this repo's own pages, and the captures live in design/evidence/. The corner probe was rebuilt to the F8 record's construction — a floor and two walls from the plane creators, a box pushed into the corner, a sphere on the floor — because the F8 harness script itself was not retained; the arrangement and the creators are the ones its record names, and design/evidence/f8-ao-probe-on.png remains the "before" it is compared against. Samples were taken on visible surfaces with the shadow state identical across modes, so the differences between modes are the occlusion, not the shadows.

The baked probe (design/evidence/f9-ao-probe-baked.png) measures like occlusion and not like a band. With nothing applied, the sampled floor points all read about 144 to 146 and the wall reads 122 at its base and near its top alike. After the bake, the floor in the wall seam drops to 53.7 and the floor strip in the corner to 58.6 — a deep, wide pool where two walls and the floor meet — the floor beside the box reads 96.9, the floor beside the smaller sphere reads 109.1, so the patch strength follows the object's actual size, the wall reads 100 near the floor and still 122 near the top, so the wall shading is a gradient that dies out with height rather than a band of fixed width, and open floor away from everything is unchanged at 146. The bake's own statistics agree with the picture: over 3,458 vertices the mean visibility is 0.863 and the minimum hits the 0.22 floor in the corner. Read back directly, the capture shows soft pooling along both wall bases and under the sphere, with the open floor clean.

The retuned screen-space pass on its own (design/evidence/f9-ao-probe-gtao-only.png, bake cleared) is deliberately mild on this scene: the seam floor reads 140.6 against 144 off, the wall base 114.3 against 122, open floor unchanged at 146. That weakness on a lone-object, grazing-angle scene is the same character the F8 record pinned on stock GTAO with a control page, and it is now acceptable precisely because the pass is no longer the static story. With both applied (design/evidence/f9-ao-probe-baked-gtao.png) the picture is the baked one; one honest wrinkle is that the composer path lifts the very darkest samples a few points against the direct path (the seam reads 57.1 combined against 53.7 baked-only), a tone-mapping-path difference between the output pass and direct rendering that was already implicit in F8 and changes nothing about where the occlusion sits.

The examples hold up. In the box example (design/evidence/f9-box-baked.png) the ground texture puts the contact patch on the default grid floor: the floor on the light side of the box, where no shadow falls, drops from 137.1 to 103.2 after the bake while open floor stays at 110. In the solar-system example (design/evidence/f9-solar-baked.png) the bake runs over all three bodies (1,683 vertices, mean visibility 0.99, minimum 0.889 — an open scene, correctly almost untouched) and open floor is unchanged at 138.7, with the sun's small contact patch visible beside its shadow in the capture.

The moving-object case is the dynamic pass's proof. The scene holds a static box and an orange box named in the bake's exclude list. With the bake alone, the floor at the orange box's light-side contact reads 146 — exactly open floor — both where the box starts and after it is moved, because an excluded object leaves no baked patch and no stain where it used to sit, while the static box's contact reads about 83 to 86 in the same captures, its baked patch present throughout. Turning the retuned chain on then drops the moved box's contact to 141.2 and changes nothing else: open floor stays 146 and the static contact stays 86.3 (design/evidence/f9-moving-gtao.png). The dynamic darkening is small — a few luminance points — and that is the honest size of the job now: it follows the object, it appears only at its contact, and it never approaches black.

## Costs

Bake cost is a build-time cost, measured in the same headless runs: the corner probe bakes 3,458 vertices with 261,548 ray casts (vertex hemispheres plus the ground texture grid) in about 0.67 seconds; the box example takes about 0.21 seconds, the moving scene about 0.28, and the solar-system scene about 0.84 — call it a fifth of a second to a second for scenes of this size on this rig, paid once when the scene is built. Frame cost, measured as wall time over real animation frames on the probe scene in the same software-rendering rig: 200.6 ms per frame direct with no bake, 301.6 ms with the bake applied, and 1,378.8 ms with the retuned chain also on. Those are SwiftShader numbers and flatter nothing, as the F8 record said of its own: the chain's roughly four-and-a-half-times multiple over the baked direct frame is a property of running full-screen shader passes on a CPU rasterizer (F8 measured the same chain at about 1,732 ms on the box scene), and on any real GPU this chain at this size is a few milliseconds. Two honest readings do survive the rig: the baked occlusion adds no per-frame pass at all (its direct-frame increase here is the tessellated geometry and the one ground quad, both trivial on a GPU), and the screen-space chain remains the expensive step, which is why it stays opt-in.

## Regression checks

The library entry still imports in Node with all 28 exports, so the standalone-consumption shape is unchanged: the bake is three new methods on PhotonicScene, not new exports, and the new internal module is imported by the scene the same way the other engine modules are. The type declarations in src/index.d.ts cover the new surface (BakedAOOptions, BakedAOInfo, the new PostProcessingOptions fields, and the blendCeiling on PostProcessingInfo), and the new methods were confirmed present on the runtime prototypes by name. The standalone host page still loads the box module through the scene loader with no page errors, and no page errors appeared in any verification run. Nothing was pushed to GitHub and nothing was published to npm; the mirror snapshot follows the usual interactive route after a roadmap-level stage.
