# F10/F11 — externally supplied geometry, and baked occlusion over it

This record covers roadmap items F10 and F11 (2026-11-12), filed by the HootCAD project. It says what was missing, what the public API is, what changed inside the bake so outside geometry gets the same treatment as engine-made shapes, and how each claim was verified, including the limits.

## What was wrong

Photonic's creators were the only way in. A consumer holding geometry of its own — HootCAD's case is JSCAD solids, which arrive in the webview as polygon soups with positions, flat normals, and a colour per part — could add a mesh straight to the raw THREE scene and watch it render, but the mesh stayed a stranger: no handle, no place in the object registry, no part in clearObjects, and no part in the baked occlusion pass, which is the shading that grounds a CAD part on its stage. HootCAD's design therefore carried a marked workaround (registering through the creators' private registration call) and filed the proper fix here.

## F10 — the public entry points

Two calls on PhotonicScene, both returning the same handle the creators return, are the whole surface.

scene.addGeometry(options) takes the data shape external geometry actually arrives in: positions (xyz triples, required), normals (optional; computed when absent), indices (optional; the soup form without them works as-is), colors (optional per-vertex colours, three or four components per vertex — the JSCAD per-part colour path, which turns on vertex colours on the created material), and uvs (optional). A ready THREE.BufferGeometry can be passed instead as options.geometry, for callers whose converter already builds one. When no material is supplied, the mesh gets a MeshStandardMaterial with the CAD-sane defaults the HootCAD preview itself uses (roughness 0.65, metalness 0.15, a neutral machined grey when no colour information exists at all, white when per-vertex colours carry the colour so they read true), and options carry the usual Photonic shape: color, roughness, metalness, opacity, transparent, doubleSided, position, rotation, scale, castShadow, receiveShadow, name, and onClick.

scene.addMesh(mesh, options) — or scene.addMesh({ mesh, ...options }) — adopts an object the caller built with THREE directly: a Mesh, a Group, or any Object3D, and a bare BufferGeometry routes to addGeometry. This is the honest route for the HootCAD bridge's actual shape of work, where the converter builds one mesh per solid and the axis change lives on a single rotated group: the group is adopted whole, its children keep their own transforms and materials, and adoption applies the scene's terms to every mesh inside it — shadow flags under the creators' rule (cast and receive on, casting off for transparent or emissive materials, both overridable per call), computed normals for any geometry that arrives without them, and the caller's position, rotation, or scale when the options give them.

Adopted geometry participates like a created object because it goes through the same registration the creators use. The handle moves, rotates, scales, recolours (a group handle recolours, and sets shadow flags on, every mesh inside it), answers getMesh() with the underlying THREE object, and removes: handle.remove(), scene.removeObject(handle), and scene.clearObjects() all detach it, and removal disposes its geometry and materials unless the call passed disposeOnRemove: false for geometry the caller still owns elsewhere. Click handlers work through the same handle, as they do for created objects.

One behaviour of the existing handles changed on the way through, deliberately: a handle's remove() now also drops the object from the scene's registry. Before this item, remove() detached and disposed but left the registry entry behind, so a removed object still counted in scene.objects and would have been fed to a later bake with a disposed geometry; removeObject already deleted the entry after calling remove(), so the fix only aligns the direct-handle path with it.

## F11 — the bake treats outside geometry as its own

Four changes inside src/PhotonicBakedAO.js and the scene's bake driver make externally supplied geometry a full citizen of scene.bakeAmbientOcclusion().

The bake now collects leaf meshes, not just top-level ones. The scene's bake driver walks every registered object and takes every mesh under it, so a mesh adopted directly and every mesh inside an adopted group both occlude and receive, exactly as separate created objects do, and handles named in the bake's exclude list exclude their whole group. The ground texture's bounds are computed from the same flattened set, so outside geometry puts its contact patch on the default floor the way a created box does.

A single solid occludes itself. The F9 bake skipped the receiving mesh when casting its rays, which is invisible for engine scenes built from separate shapes but wrong for a CAD solid, whose boss, walls, and bore are one mesh: under the old rule a merged part would receive almost nothing. Rays now test every occluder including the receiver. The origin offset along the normal that F9 already used keeps convex surfaces clean (a lone convex part still bakes to full visibility, verified below), and concave features — the demo part's counterbore floor reads 0.365 mean visibility against 0.782 on the open plate top of the same mesh — shade themselves the way separate meshes shade each other.

Vertex colours are darkened, not replaced. The F9 bake wrote its visibility grey into the colour channel, which would have erased the per-part colours a JSCAD model arrives with. The bake now keeps any colour attribute a geometry arrives with, stores a copy, writes base colour times visibility per vertex (alpha untouched on four-component colours), and clearing the bake puts the original colours back exactly — the verification below measured a maximum delta of zero across the part's whole colour array after a bake and clear. Geometry carrying its own colours is also exempt from the bake's tessellation step, which subdivides only the engine's authored planes and boxes; subdividing coloured geometry would throw its colours away, and CAD meshes arrive already tessellated at the resolution their author chose. The honest consequence: per-vertex occlusion on external geometry resolves at the caller's own vertex density, so a very coarse mesh gets coarse occlusion on its faces while its contact patches, which live in the ground texture and in its neighbours' finer vertices, stay exact.

The ray casts run against a triangle index, not against THREE meshes. F9's per-ray cost was a THREE.Raycaster walk over every triangle of every nearby mesh, which was fine for the engine's low-poly shapes and impossible for a CAD mesh: the 38,800-triangle measurement below would have needed on the order of twenty billion triangle tests. The bake now flattens the occluders' world-space triangles once, builds a small BVH over them (median splits on the longest centroid axis, leaves of eight triangles, a double-sided Möller–Trumbore test so wall planes block from either face exactly as the old double-sided proxy materials did), and answers each ray with a walk that touches tens of triangles. getBakedAOInfo() reports the index's triangle count alongside the F9 statistics. The sample directions, weighting, radius, and visibility floor are unchanged, and the F9 corner probe re-baked through the new path reads the same character as its F9 record (below).

Costs, measured in Node on this machine and stated as the one-time build cost they are: the demo scene (five objects, 466 triangles, 1,076 receiving vertices, the full 128 by 128 ground texture) bakes in about 0.2 seconds in the headless browser. A CAD-sized turned part of 38,800 triangles standing on a floor, 21,083 receiving vertices and 618,268 rays including the ground pass, bakes in about 1.3 seconds. A stress case of 193,200 occluder triangles and 78,995 receiving vertices takes about 3.8 seconds. So the order is the F9 order — tenths of a second for stage-sized scenes, a few seconds for a dense CAD model — paid once per rebuild, never per frame. A HootCAD-sized model that re-bakes on every parameter change should expect that few-seconds figure at the top of its range, which is why the HootCAD design's plan to show the stage unbaked and land the bake when it lands remains the right shape.

## Verification

Everything below ran headlessly in Chromium with software rendering against examples/custom-geometry-demo/, which builds its part the way JSCAD data actually arrives: a soup of convex polygons for a plate, a boss with a counterbore, and a step pad, each with one flat colour, fan-triangulated with flat normals exactly as HootCAD's webview converter does, then handed to addGeometry as positions, normals, and per-vertex colours. The same page adopts a caller-built group turned −90 degrees about X with a knob inside it (the HootCAD bridge's axis change) through addMesh, and a raw indexed BoxGeometry with its normals deleted through addMesh. Twenty-four checks pass, with no page errors: the registry holds all five objects; the part's flags, vertex count (654, matching the soup builder), colours, and stage position are as built; the group turn puts the knob upright at the expected world position; the indexed geometry keeps its index and gains computed normals; handle transforms move the part and a group handle's shadow switch reaches the child mesh; the bake reports five receiving meshes, 466 occluder triangles, mean visibility 0.707, and 5,725 shaded ground texels; colour ratios survive the bake; the bore floor is darker than the plate top in the same mesh; the plinth beside the models darkens from 16.2 to 12.9 after the bake while open floor far away is unchanged at 110.1; clearing restores colours exactly; and removal, group removal, and clearObjects all behave. Shadow participation was verified by reading the captures directly: the before capture (design/evidence/f10-part-before.png) shows real shadows at the boss's base, under the pad, and beside the knob with no bake applied, and the after capture (design/evidence/f11-part-baked.png) shows the same scene with the bore's interior gone dark, contact darkening pooled at the boss base and under the pad, and the plinth around the part's footprint shaded. An automated pixel probe for the shadow itself was attempted and dropped rather than reported: from the demo camera the boss's shadow falls on the far side of the boss, and the probe points that are geometrically in shadow are occluded by the boss itself, so the check could not be made honest — the flag state and the read-back captures carry the claim instead.

Regression checks: the F9 corner probe, rebuilt on the same page through the creators and baked through the new triangle-index path, reports mean visibility 0.846 and minimum 0.244, against the F9 record's 0.863 and 0.22 floor on its slightly different probe construction — the same deep-pool character through the new code. A lone convex cylinder bakes to mean visibility 1.0 in Node, so self-occlusion does not shade convex solids. The library entry still imports in Node with all 28 exports (the features are new scene methods, not new exports), and a strict TypeScript 5 consumer using addGeometry, addMesh in both forms, getMesh, and the bake's triangle count compiles clean against src/index.d.ts.

## Limits

Per-vertex occlusion resolves at the geometry's own vertex density, as stated above; a coarse CAD mesh that wants finer shading on its own faces must subdivide before it arrives, and the ground texture and neighbouring objects still ground it correctly in the meantime. The ground texture is laid on the default floor, so a part standing on a raised plinth gets its contact shading from the plinth's own baked vertices (which the demo shows working) rather than from the texture. addMesh applies the creators' shadow defaults at adoption; a caller who set different flags by hand should pass castShadow and receiveShadow in the adoption options to keep them. Nothing was pushed to GitHub and nothing was published to npm; the release decision is Joel's, and the docs site's vendored library copy refreshes through docs/refresh-vendored.mjs when the release is cut, as it did for 0.2.0.
